Patches, attention and Vision Transformers

A convolutional network is built on one assumption: what matters is nearby. A Vision Transformer drops that assumption entirely. It chops the image into patches, lines them up as a sequence, and lets every patch look at every other patch from the first layer onward. Below is a real one, single head, written by hand, trained live on this page, with its attention maps left open.

Step 1 · An image becomes a sequence

A transformer has no idea what an image is. It handles sequences of vectors, so the first job is turning a picture into one: slice it into a grid of non-overlapping squares, flatten each square into a list of numbers, multiply by a learned matrix. A 20×20 image with 4×4 patches becomes 25 tokens.

Flattening throws away every trace of where things were. Token 7 has no idea it came from the middle of the second row. So the model adds a learned position embedding to each one, a small vector meaning "I am patch 7". Position isn't structural in a transformer. It's a fact the model gets told and then has to learn to use.

Patch size is the main trade you make in a ViT. Big patches give you few tokens and cheap attention, but each token is a blurry summary. Small patches catch fine detail and cost a fortune, because attention compares every token against every other one. Halving the patch size multiplies the attention work by roughly sixteen.

Step 2 · What attention computes

Each token produces three vectors by multiplying itself with three learned matrices:

Query

What am I looking for? Emitted by the token doing the looking.

Key

What do I contain? A label other tokens can match against.

Value

What do I pass on if someone attends to me?

Compare every query against every key with a dot product, scale it, and softmax the result into weights that sum to one. Each token's output is then the weighted average of everyone's values:

attention(Q, K, V)  =  softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d ) · V

That's the whole mechanism: a soft, differentiable lookup. Find the tokens whose keys match my query and blend their values together. Nothing in it knows about images, neighbourhoods or edges, which is precisely why the same operation turns up unchanged in language models.

Why √d?

Dot products of d-dimensional vectors grow with d. Skip the scaling and the scores get big, softmax saturates into a hard one-hot pick, and the gradient through it all but disappears. Dividing by √d keeps the scores in the range where softmax stays soft enough to train. One symbol, one real training failure averted.

Step 3 · Train it and read the attention

Same digit generator as the CNN lesson, so the comparison is fair. Train it, then click around the image and the purple overlay shows where attention flows. Before training the maps are near-uniform noise, every patch mildly interested in everything. Afterwards they have structure. Expect about 60% after thirty seconds and 85% or so after two minutes. That's slower than the CNN, and the gap is the lesson rather than a bug.

Training

Configuration

Three real design choices, all switchable. The class token is an extra learned token that attends to every patch and whose output feeds the classifier, which is the arrangement from the ViT paper; the alternative just averages the patch tokens. LayerNorm normalises each token before each block and is what makes deep stacks trainable at all. Both ship off here, because at this size both measurably slow learning down. Heads splits the embedding into independent slices that each run their own comparison, so different heads can chase different relationships instead of averaging everything into one map. Switch to 2 or 4 and a strip appears under the attention panel showing each head separately. Turn any of them on and watch what happens; that comparison is most of the point of this panel.

Step 4 · What the position embeddings learned

Each tile below belongs to one patch position and shows how similar that position's learned embedding is to every other position's. Red is similar, blue is dissimilar.

At the start these are random static. As training goes on, each tile usually grows a bright spot at its own location with a soft glow around it. The model has worked out which patches are neighbours, from nothing but the data. It had to learn the grid layout that a convolution is handed for free by its architecture, and that's the trade the whole approach rests on: fewer built-in assumptions, more that has to be learned, and a much bigger appetite for data as a result.

CNN or transformer?

On this page the CNN wins, and it deserves to. A thousand tiny images is exactly the regime where built-in assumptions pay off. A convolution starts life already knowing that nearby pixels belong together and that a pattern means the same thing wherever it shows up. The transformer starts knowing neither and has to learn both from examples.

Flip the data budget and the ranking flips with it. Given hundreds of millions of images, having no assumptions turns into freedom: the transformer can learn relationships a 3×3 kernel structurally cannot express, like the top-left of an image informing the bottom-right in the very first layer. That's the actual finding of the ViT paper, and it's why both architectures are still around.

What's missing here, honestly

A production ViT has several attention heads per layer (this has one), a dozen or more stacked blocks (this has one), dropout, and a training run measured in GPU-months. What's here is the load-bearing idea with the scaffolding stripped off. It does genuinely learn, clearing 85% on ten classes given a couple of minutes. Hold that next to the CNN, which hits 99% on the same data in about twenty seconds.

Take the switches above seriously, because they taught me something while I was building this. A class token, LayerNorm and multiple attention heads are all standard, all clearly correct at scale, and all three make this particular model learn slower. I set four heads as the default at first, since that's what you do. Then I measured it. On a fixed 40-second budget from an identical seed, one head reached 92%. Two reached 72%. Four reached 69%. Same parameter count in every case, because splitting 24 features across four heads leaves each comparison squinting at the world through six dimensions.

The machinery that's essential for a 12-block model on a hundred million images is dead weight on a 1-block model on three thousand. Best practice is always best practice at some scale, and the scale usually goes unmentioned because everyone in the room already shares it. The switches are there so you can check that on something small rather than take my word for it.

Experiments worth running

  • Look at the attention before training, then after. Untrained maps are flat, every patch attending to everything about equally. Trained maps concentrate. That change is the model working out what's worth looking at.
  • Watch "what the classifier reads" after training. The bright patches usually land on the digit's strokes and not the empty background. Nobody labelled the strokes. Attention found them because that's where the information is.
  • Run the ablation yourself. Fix the seed, pin the curve, flip exactly one switch, and give it the same time from a fresh reset. The pinned dashed curve is your control. That's an ablation study, and it's the same reasoning that decides what goes into a real architecture.
  • Switch to 4 heads and watch the strip. Every head gets its own map, and with six dimensions apiece they mostly end up looking the same. The head specialisation you read about in papers needs far more width per head than this model has to hand out.
  • Set patches to 10×10 (a 2×2 grid). Four tokens is barely any spatial resolution and accuracy falls over. Then try 2×2 patches: 100 tokens, a pile more attention arithmetic, slower training, and no obvious improvement at this size.
  • Shrink the embedding to 8. Queries and keys now live in eight dimensions, tokens can barely tell each other apart, and attention goes muddy.
  • Draw a digit far off centre. Compare how the CNN and the transformer handle it. Neither is truly translation invariant at the output, but they break in different ways.
  • Train, then set augmentation to 0 and regenerate. Test accuracy jumps. The model didn't get better, the exam got easier. Always ask what a benchmark number is actually measuring.
  • Drop the training images to 250, then push it to 6,000. (Press "New data" after moving the slider.) The CNN hardly notices. This model cares enormously, and that gap is the whole practical argument between the two architectures.
