Search + self-play · AlphaZero

Teach a model to search

Every other agent on this site reacts. It reads the situation, picks a move and lives with it. This one stops and thinks: before each move it plays out hundreds of imagined continuations, and the network's job is not to pick the move but to guess what all that thinking will conclude. It learns the game by playing itself, and nobody ever tells it a single rule of strategy.

Thinking time is how many games it plays out in its head before each move, and it is the single biggest lever on how hard this is. Measured on the finished agent, forty games against the same scripted bot: 23 wins with the search off, 36 at twenty-five simulations, 39 at a hundred. Same weights in all three.

What it is considering

One bar per column, showing how much of its thinking budget went there. The search spends its time where the results are good, so the tallest bar is the move it will play — and the second tallest is the move it took seriously and rejected.

Why this cannot be done the usual way

In the Rocket League lesson the agent learns a reflex: a number arrives thirty times a second and it adjusts. Connect 4 gives you nothing like that. One number arrives at the very end of the game, win or lose, and by then forty moves have gone by and you have no idea which of them mattered. Worse, a position that looks fine is often lost already, several moves before anything visibly goes wrong.

So instead of guessing harder, you look. The agent runs a search over what might happen next, guided by the network but not limited to it, and that search sees the trap the network missed. Then the network is trained on what the search concluded, which makes the next search better.

network guides search  →  search plays better than network  →  train network on search  →  repeat

That loop is the whole of AlphaZero, and it is the reason a program can get superhuman at a game nobody taught it. The network is a fast approximation of a slow, careful process, and each round of training makes the approximation good enough to make the careful process cheaper.

Watch it think

Press the button and the search runs one simulation at a time so you can see the tree being built. Each simulation walks down from the current position choosing promising moves, stops somewhere it has not been, asks the network what it thinks, and carries that verdict back up the path it came down. Nothing is random about which branches get explored — it is the formula below, and the two halves of it pull in opposite directions.

One simulation at a time

pick the move with the largest   Q(a)  +  c · P(a) · √N ⁄ (1 + N(a))

Q(a) is how well this move has actually turned out so far. P(a) is what the network guessed before any of it was tried. Early on the second term dominates and the search follows the network's hunch; as visits pile up the denominator grows and results take over. That is the whole balance between trusting a model and checking it.

Train your own

This starts from random weights on your machine. It plays itself, labels every position with what the search decided there and who eventually won, and trains on that. There is no dataset and no opponent: the only teacher is a slightly better version of the same agent.

1 · Play

The current network plays a full game against itself, running a tree search at every move and sampling its choice from the visit counts so no two games are the same.

2 · Label

Each position is stored with the search's move distribution, and once the game ends, with the result from that player's point of view.

3 · Learn

Two heads, two losses: match the search's preferences, and predict the winner. Then the improved network goes back to step 1.

Progress

Policy loss starts at ln 7 = 1.95, which is what a network with no opinion costs. Value loss often climbs partway through, and that is not a fault: as the agent gets better its games get closer, and predicting the winner of a close game is genuinely harder.

Hyperparameters

Self-play scores are meaningless

The agent is both players, so it wins exactly half its games no matter how good or bad it is. "Test it" plays the fixed scripted opponent instead — one that always takes a win and always blocks a loss — which is the only number here that can go up.

Getting better is not a straight line

Here is every checkpoint from the run that ships with this page, each one played forty times against the same scripted bot. I expected a curve that climbs. What it actually does is fall off a cliff first.

The untrained network wins 30 of 40, because a tree search with no knowledge at all still notices a win one move away. Two hundred and seventy-eight games later it wins 1. It takes until around two thousand five hundred games just to get back to where it started, and six thousand to finish clearly ahead at 35.

The reason is worth sitting with, because it is the opposite of the usual intuition. A network with no opinion lets the search look everywhere. A network with a confident wrong opinion points the search at one column and it stops checking the others. Half-learned is worse than ignorant here, and the only cure is more training. So "trained longer" is not a difficulty setting, which is why the rungs under the board are ordered by measured results and their games-played numbers do not climb in step.

How much of it is the network?

Worth being precise about. I took the finished agent — the strongest rung, six thousand games — and played it against the scripted bot forty times at four different search budgets, changing nothing else:

With the search switched off the network is not hopeless: 23 of 40 is a winning record against a bot that always takes a win and always blocks one. But turn the search on and the same weights take 39 of 40. Everything between those two numbers is search, not learning.

You can see what each half contributes. Ask the shipped network about an empty board and it puts 91% of its probability on the centre column, which is correct and is a genuinely useful thing to know about Connect 4 — it worked that out from six thousand games with nobody mentioning it. Then set up positions where one move wins immediately: the raw network picks the winning move in about a quarter of them. It has absorbed where good positions come from. It has not absorbed how to count to four, and it never will, because looking is cheaper than remembering.

Experiments worth running

  • Drag thinking time down to 1. The search is gone and you are playing the raw network. It plays the centre, it has opinions about shape, and it will walk straight into a four you set up in plain sight.
  • Play the weakest rung at high thinking time. Its network is actively misleading — it is the checkpoint from the collapse — and yet it is still awkward to beat, because the search keeps rescuing it from its own advice.
  • Set up a three-in-a-row and watch the bars. The column that blocks you takes almost the entire budget. That is the search discovering a threat the network did not flag.
  • Train from scratch and test every thirty seconds. The first improvement is positional — it stops throwing discs at the edges — and the tactics arrive much later.
  • Turn the Dirichlet noise off and train. Self-play games become near-identical, the buffer fills with one opening, and progress stalls. It is the only exploration this algorithm has.
  • Compare with the Rocket League agent. Same idea of learning from your own play, completely different machinery: one reacts in milliseconds, this one deliberates. Which you can afford depends entirely on whether the clock is running.
