Attention · where it came from

Teach a model to write

The attention block from the Vision Transformer lesson wasn't invented for images. Here it is aimed at what it was built for: text, one character at a time. Give it a page of writing and press train. The samples go from noise, to letters, to words, to something that almost reads like a sentence, in about a minute, on your machine, out of ten thousand numbers.

Text is just a sequence of symbols

The model has no concept of words, spelling or grammar. It sees a list of character IDs taken from whatever characters turn up in your text, and its entire job is one question asked once per position: given the last few characters, what comes next? That's a classification problem with one class per character.

Everything that later looks like knowledge falls out of getting that one question right often enough. That q is followed by u. That a full stop is followed by a space and a capital. That "the" is a very popular way to start. None of it is stored anywhere as a rule.

The corpus

edit it, or paste your own writing

Anything works, but short and heavily patterned works best. A ten-thousand-parameter model finds structure long before it finds meaning, so a recipe beats a novel. Your text never leaves the page.

The vocabulary

every distinct character, and its ID

Real language models use sub-word tokens instead, a few tens of thousands of fragments like ing or ▁the, which makes sequences much shorter and training much cheaper. Characters are the honest thing to learn on. There's no tokenizer to hide behind and you can read every ID in the table.

Train it

Press train and read the sample box as it goes. Nonsense for a few seconds. Then plausible letter pairs. Then real words with the grammar still broken. Then whole clauses that have picked up the rhythm of your text. The loss underneath is measured in nats per character: it starts near ln(vocabulary), which is what pure guessing costs, and every drop from there is the model finding some structure it can use.

Progress

At a loss of 1.0 the model is about as uncertain per character as a two-and-a-half-way coin flip. Below that it's writing recognisable words.

What it is actually deciding

At every step the model produces a probability for each character in the vocabulary, and one gets drawn at random. Type in the prompt box and watch the list rearrange itself. After training, "th" makes "e" overwhelming and a full stop makes a space nearly certain.

Temperature reshapes exactly this list before the draw. Low temperature sharpens it toward the single likeliest character, which is why a cold model eventually gets stuck repeating itself forever.

Causal attention

rows look along their own row only

The map is a triangle, and the triangle is the rule: position t may look at positions 0 through t and nothing after. Take the mask away and the model can read the answer it's being asked to predict. The loss drops to zero, you feel briefly excellent about yourself, and the samples are garbage.

How this differs from the models you've heard of

What's on this page is the real architecture: embeddings, causal self-attention, a residual stream, layer norm, a feed-forward block, softmax over the vocabulary. A production language model is that same diagram with three things changed, and not one of them is a new idea.

  • Scale. This has roughly ten thousand parameters and one block. A frontier model has hundreds of billions and a hundred or more blocks. Same shape, stacked and widened until it stops fitting on one machine.
  • Tokens. Sub-word pieces rather than characters, so a thousand words is about a thousand tokens instead of five thousand characters.
  • Data. Your corpus here is a few thousand characters. Theirs is measured in trillions of tokens, and that's where nearly everything resembling knowledge comes from.
The uncomfortable part

Watch your model write a sentence that's grammatical and entirely made up. It has no notion of truth. It's sampling a plausible next character, over and over, and a plausible sentence is what that adds up to. Everything unsettling about how large language models fail is visible right here in miniature, at a size where you can see exactly why it happens.

Experiments worth running

  • Drop the context to 8 characters and train. Spelling survives. Sentence structure doesn't. The model literally cannot see far enough back to know where it is in a sentence.
  • Set the temperature to 0.1, then 1.5. One repeats a single phrase until you stop it, the other invents spellings. The readable band between them is narrower than you'd expect and worth finding by hand.
  • Paste in your own writing. An email, some notes, anything a few thousand characters long. A model this small will parrot your habits back at you: your favourite sentence openings, your punctuation, your tics. It's a slightly uncomfortable mirror.
  • Train on the recipe, then prompt with "heat the". It has picked up the grammar of instructions without anyone mentioning that instructions exist.
  • Pin the loss curve, halve the embedding size, and train again. Same seed, same data, one change. That's a controlled experiment about model capacity, and it takes ninety seconds.
  • Watch the attention map after training. Find the row for a character that comes after a space. It usually attends back to the start of the word it's currently in the middle of.
