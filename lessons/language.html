Attention · where it came from

Teach a model to write

The attention block from the Vision Transformer lesson was not invented for images. Here it is pointed at its original target: text, one character at a time. Give it a page of writing, press train, and watch the samples go from noise to letters to words to something that reads almost like a sentence — in about a minute, on your machine, from ten thousand numbers.

Text is just a sequence of symbols

The model has no notion of words, spelling or grammar. It sees a list of character IDs, drawn from whatever characters appear in your corpus, and its whole job is: given the last few characters, which character comes next? That's a classification problem with one class per character, asked once per position.

Everything that looks like knowledge afterwards — that q is followed by u, that a full stop is followed by a space and a letter, that "the" is a very common way to start — is a consequence of getting that one question right often enough.

The corpus

edit it, or paste your own writing

Anything works, but short and heavily patterned works best — a ten-thousand-parameter model learns structure long before it learns meaning. Your text never leaves the page.

The vocabulary

every distinct character, and its ID

Real language models use sub-word tokens instead — a few tens of thousands of fragments like ing or ▁the — which makes sequences far shorter and training far cheaper. Characters are the honest version to learn on: no tokenizer to hide behind, and you can read every ID in the table.

Train it

Press train and read the sample box. Expect nonsense for a few seconds, then plausible letter pairs, then real words with broken grammar, then whole clauses copied from the rhythm of your corpus. The loss you're watching is measured in nats per character: it starts near ln(vocabulary) — pure guessing — and every drop is the model finding structure.

Progress

A loss of 1.0 means the model is, on average, about as uncertain as a two-and-a-half-way coin flip at each character. Below that it is writing recognisable words.

What it is actually deciding

At every step the model produces a probability for each character in the vocabulary, and one is drawn at random. Type in the prompt box and watch the distribution change — after training, th makes e overwhelming, and a full stop makes a space almost certain.

Temperature reshapes exactly this list before the draw. Low temperature sharpens it toward the single most likely character — which is why it eventually repeats itself forever.

Causal attention

rows look along their own row only

The map is a triangle, and that shape is the rule: position t may attend to positions 0…t and nothing later. Without it, the model could read the answer it was being asked to predict, the loss would collapse to zero, and the samples would be garbage.

How this differs from the models you've heard of

What's on this page is the real architecture: embeddings, causal self-attention, a residual stream, layer norm, a feed-forward block, and a softmax over the vocabulary. A production language model is the same diagram with three things changed, and none of them is a new idea.

  • Scale. This has around ten thousand parameters and one block. A frontier model has hundreds of billions and a hundred or more blocks — the same shape, stacked and widened until it stops fitting on a single machine.
  • Tokens. Sub-word pieces instead of characters, so a sequence of a thousand words is a thousand-odd tokens rather than five thousand characters.
  • Data. Your corpus here is a few thousand characters. Theirs is measured in trillions of tokens, which is where almost everything that looks like knowledge comes from.
The uncomfortable part

Watch your model produce a sentence that is grammatical and completely made up. It has no notion of truth — it is sampling a plausible next character, repeatedly. Everything unsettling about how large language models fail is visible in miniature here, at a scale where you can see exactly why it happens.

Experiments worth running

  • Drop the context to 8 characters and train. Spelling survives; sentence structure does not. The model literally cannot see far enough back to know where it is.
  • Set the temperature to 0.1, then 1.5. One repeats a single phrase forever, the other invents spellings. The readable range is narrow and worth finding by hand.
  • Paste in your own writing — an email, some notes, anything a few thousand characters long. A model this small will parrot your habits: your favourite sentence openings, your punctuation, your tics.
  • Train on the recipe, then prompt with "heat the". It has learned the grammar of instructions without being told there is such a thing.
  • Pin the loss curve, halve the embedding size, and train again. Same seed, same data, one change — a controlled experiment about capacity.
  • Watch the attention map after training. Look at the row for a character that follows a space: it usually attends back to the start of the word it is in the middle of.
