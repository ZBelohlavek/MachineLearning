Cooking with a stranger

Every other agent on this site had someone to beat. This one has someone to help, and that turns out to be a different problem. Two cooks share one kitchen and one score, and the only way to fail is to get in each other's way. Train a pair by self-play and they become good partners — for each other, specifically.

Collisions are the coordination cost, counted every tick you both want the same square. A well-matched pair has very few. A mismatched pair spends the shift shuffling.

The result this lesson exists for

Two agents were trained here by identical self-play, differing only in random seed. Each played thousands of shifts against a copy of itself. Then I paired them with somebody else, and measured what happened rather than assuming it.

Both agents beat two by-the-book cooks when working with a copy of themselves. Put either next to a cook it has never met and it falls back to roughly what the by-the-book pair already managed — about a quarter of its advantage survives. Put the two trained agents together and they do worse than two plain scripted cooks, which is the part I did not expect: two individually strong players, each with a plan, and the plans do not fit.

The advantage they lose is a convention. Self-play optimises a pair, and a pair is free to agree on anything: who fetches, who plates, which way round the counter everyone walks. The agreement is worth real points and costs nothing to make, so it gets made — and none of it survives contact with anyone who was not at the meeting.

Four of six runs never learned at all

Worth knowing before you train your own. With identical settings and only the seed changed, four of the six runs behind this page plateaued serving one soup a shift and never improved; two reached six or better. The two good ones are what ships. That is ordinary practice and rarely mentioned, which is exactly why it is mentioned here: a single run tells you very little about a method on a task with a reward chain this long.

Why this matters beyond a kitchen

Anything meant to work alongside a person hits this: a driving policy trained against copies of itself, a negotiating agent trained against itself, a recommender trained against a model of a user rather than a user. Each learns a convention with whoever it trained against, and a person is not that. The usual fix is to train against a deliberately varied population of partners, which costs more and gives up some of the top-end score in exchange for a partner that works with anyone.

Train one yourself

Two cooks, one shared network, one score. It learns from a shaped reward: a little for picking up the right thing, more for filling the pot, a lot for serving. The chain from an empty kitchen to a served soup is six steps, which is far too long to stumble into by accident, and that shapes everything about how it has to be taught.

Progress

Hyperparameters

The exploration bonus is not optional here

With the entropy bonus set to zero this policy commits to a habit within a few hundred episodes and never serves a single soup, however long you leave it. Turn it down to zero and watch: the score line flattens at nothing. Six-step reward chains do not forgive an agent that stops trying things.

Experiments worth running

  • Play a shift with each partner in turn. The self-play agent expects a particular dance and keeps walking into you when you don't do it. The by-the-book cook is duller and easier to work with. Watch the collision counter more than the score: the relationship shows up there first.
  • Stand still for a whole shift. See how many soups your partner manages alone. The self-play agents are notably bad at this: they learned to rely on a second pair of hands that always did the right half of the job.
  • Set the entropy bonus to zero and train. The score line never leaves the floor. This is the clearest demonstration on the site of exploration being load-bearing rather than decorative.
  • Train two of your own with different seeds using the seed control, then test the pairings. You should see the same collapse, on agents you made.
  • Watch the collision counter rather than the score. It tells you about the relationship rather than the result, and it moves first — a mismatched pair is colliding long before the score gap is obvious.
