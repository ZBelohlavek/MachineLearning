Optimising without a gradient

Build a production line: ore from the mine, plates from a smelter, gears from an assembler, out through the depot. What is being optimised here is not a set of weights but a layout — a pile of discrete choices with no derivative anywhere in it. You cannot differentiate a conveyor belt. So how do you improve one?

The recipe

The mine pushes one ore a tick into whatever is next to it. A belt carries its item one square a tick in the direction it faces. A smelter turns ore into a plate in four ticks; an assembler turns a plate into a gear in six. Gears that reach the depot score. Anything that walks off the end of a belt is lost.

What happened

Ore smelted and plates made are worth watching even when no gears come out. They are the only sign that the first half of your line works, and they are exactly the signal the search below needs in order to get anywhere.

There is nothing here to differentiate

Every other lesson on this site improves something by asking which way to nudge it. That question has no answer here. A belt is either there or not; it faces one of four directions; an assembler cannot be moved half a square. The objective is the output of a simulation, and the space of layouts is discrete, enormous and mostly worthless.

Worse, it is almost entirely flat. A layout one assembler short of working produces exactly what an empty map produces: nothing. There is no slope leading towards a solution, because until the last piece is in place every partial factory scores the same zero.

Which is the sparse-reward problem again

The Rocket League agent could not learn from goals alone because a goal arrives once in three hundred decisions. This is the same shape of problem with no learning in it at all: the search needed partial credit for getting ore into a smelter and plates out of one, or it never found a single working factory. Shaping is not a reinforcement learning trick. It is what you do whenever the thing you actually want is too rare to steer by.

So change things at random and keep what helps

Simulated annealing, which is about as old as computing and still hard to beat here. Make a random edit. If it helped, keep it. If it hurt, keep it anyway with a probability that starts high and falls — because a layout that is locally fine and globally mediocre can only be escaped by accepting something worse on the way out.

Does the search actually earn its keep?

Worth checking against the obvious alternative: generate a great many random layouts and keep the best one. Both approaches are given the same simulator and the same budget.

Trying twenty thousand random layouts, a few hundred at a time, so the page stays usable while it runs…

Random sampling finds nothing whatsoever, and would keep finding nothing however long you ran it, because working factories are a vanishingly thin slice of the space. The annealer finds one every time. The difference between them is not cleverness — it is that one of them keeps partial progress and builds on it, and the other throws away everything it has learned after each attempt.

The move set mattered more than the algorithm

Two changes made the difference between a search that worked and one that didn't, and neither was a tuning parameter. First, a move that builds a belt pointing onwards from a piece that already exists, rather than dropping one anywhere. Second, letting a placement clear cheap belts elsewhere to pay for itself — without that, layouts filled up with belts and could never afford the assembler they needed, and the search happily polished a factory that could never produce a gear.

Experiments worth running

  • Build a line that makes plates but no gears. Run it. The throughput is zero, and it is exactly as zero as an empty map — which is what the search is up against.
  • Beat the optimiser. It usually lands between 14 and 17 gears per hundred ticks. A tight hand-built line with the machines packed close together can do better, because every belt you remove is budget you can spend on another machine.
  • Halve the budget. The problem changes character completely: with very little to spend, the search has to find the shortest possible path, and the answer stops looking like the one a person would draw.
  • Turn the temperature to zero. That is plain hill climbing. It gets stuck in the first decent layout it stumbles into and stays there.
  • Watch the plateaus on the chart. Thousands of edits with no improvement, then a jump. That is the shape of searching a space where most of the neighbours of a good answer are worthless.
