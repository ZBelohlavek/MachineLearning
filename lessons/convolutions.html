How a computer sees an edge

A vision model doesn't see a cat. It sees a grid of brightness numbers. Everything it eventually knows is built out of one almost embarrassingly simple operation, repeated millions of times: multiply a small patch of those numbers by a small grid of weights, and add them up. Do it by hand here, one pixel at a time, and most of the mystery drains out of computer vision.

The operation

Take a 3×3 window of the image. Take a 3×3 grid of weights, which is called the kernel. Multiply them element by element, add up the nine results, and write that one number into the output at the window's centre. Slide across one pixel and do it again, for every pixel in the image. That's a convolution. There genuinely isn't any more to it.

All the magic sits in the weights. A kernel with −1 down the left and +1 down the right computes "how much brighter is the right side than the left?", which is zero everywhere the image is flat and large exactly where a vertical edge is. You didn't program an edge detector. You wrote down six numbers and an edge detector fell out.

For edge kernels the output is centred on mid-grey: no response is grey, a strong positive is white, a strong negative is black. An edge detector never says "edge here". It says "brightness is changing here, in this direction, by this much", and everything downstream has to make do with that.

A real convolutional layer does precisely this: run a whole bank of kernels over the same input and stack the results into a pile of feature maps, one per kernel. The only difference is that it doesn't use anybody's hand-written numbers. It learns its own.

Three details that matter later

Padding, or what happens at the border

The window at pixel (0,0) hangs off the edge of the image, and something has to fill those empty cells. This page repeats the nearest edge pixel. Other implementations pad with zeros, or refuse to compute the border at all, which quietly shrinks the output by two pixels per convolution. It sounds like a footnote right up until you stack twenty layers and go looking for your missing image.

Stride, or how far the window jumps

Sliding one pixel at a time gives you an output the same size as the input. Jump two pixels instead, which is stride 2, and the output is half the size in each direction. It's the cheapest way there is to shrink a representation as it moves deeper into a network.

Pooling, or throwing away the details on purpose

Max pooling takes each 2×2 block and keeps only the biggest value. You lose exactly where inside the block the response happened, and losing it is the entire point. After pooling, "there's a vertical edge around here" survives and "at precisely this pixel" doesn't. That's how a network stops caring whether a digit was drawn two pixels to the left.

Experiments worth running

  • Type your own kernel. Put 1 across the top row, −1 across the bottom, zeros in the middle. You've just built a horizontal edge detector. Now flip the signs and watch the output invert: the filter has a direction and it cares which side is bright.
  • Make every weight the same small positive number. That's a blur, and the reason is worth sitting with. Averaging a neighbourhood destroys exactly the high-frequency detail an edge kernel exists to find. Blur and edge-detect are opposites.
  • Run Sobel X on the diagonal stripes, then Sobel Y. Same image, completely different maps. One kernel can't see everything, which is why real layers carry dozens of them.
  • Try the noise image with a blur, then with sharpen. Sharpening noise gives you louder noise. A filter has no idea which parts are signal.
  • Draw a thick white line, then a thin one. Compare the Laplacian's response to each. Kernel size sets the scale of feature a layer can even perceive, and a 3×3 kernel is blind to anything wider than three pixels. Stacking is how networks get around that.
  • Upload a photo of yourself. Sobel X on a face is a genuinely odd thing to look at, and it's roughly the first thing every vision model in the world computes about you.
