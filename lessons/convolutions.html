Computer vision · foundations

How a computer sees an edge

A vision model doesn't see a cat. It sees a grid of brightness numbers. Everything it eventually knows is built out of one absurdly simple operation repeated millions of times: multiply a small patch of those numbers by a small grid of weights and add them up. Do it here, one pixel at a time, and the rest of computer vision stops being mysterious.

The operation

Take a 3×3 window of the image. Take a 3×3 grid of weights — the kernel. Multiply them together element by element, add up the nine results, and write that single number into the output image at the window's centre. Slide the window one pixel across and repeat, for every pixel in the image. That's a convolution. There is nothing else to it.

The magic is entirely in the weights. A kernel with −1 on the left and +1 on the right computes "how much brighter is the right side than the left?" — which is zero everywhere the image is flat, and large exactly where a vertical edge is. You didn't program an edge detector; you wrote down six numbers and an edge detector fell out.

For edge kernels the output is centred on mid-grey: zero response is grey, a strong positive response is white and a strong negative one is black. An edge detector doesn't say "edge here", it says "the brightness is changing here, in this direction, by this much".

The same image through six kernels

A real convolutional layer does exactly this: it runs a whole bank of kernels over the same input and stacks the results into a pile of feature maps, one per kernel. The difference is that it doesn't use anyone's hand-written numbers — it learns its own.

Three details that matter later

Padding, or what happens at the border

The window at pixel (0,0) hangs off the edge of the image. Something has to fill those cells: this page repeats the nearest edge pixel, others pad with zeros or refuse to compute the border at all (which shrinks the output by two pixels per convolution). It sounds like a footnote until you stack twenty layers and discover your image has quietly shrunk away.

Stride, or how far the window jumps

Sliding one pixel at a time gives an output the same size as the input. Jump two pixels at a time — stride 2 — and the output is half the size in each direction. It's the cheapest way to shrink a representation as it moves deeper into a network.

Pooling, or throwing away the details on purpose

Max pooling takes each 2×2 block and keeps only the largest value. You lose exactly where within the block the response happened, and that loss is the point: after pooling, "there's a vertical edge around here" survives while "at precisely this pixel" doesn't. That's how a network becomes tolerant of a digit being drawn a couple of pixels to the left.

Experiments worth running

  • Type your own kernel. Put 1 in the top row, −1 in the bottom, zeros in the middle. You've just built a horizontal edge detector. Now flip the signs and watch the output invert: the filter has a direction, and it cares which side is bright.
  • Make every weight the same small positive number. That's a blur, and the reason is worth sitting with: averaging a neighbourhood destroys exactly the high-frequency detail an edge kernel is looking for. Blur and edge-detect are opposites.
  • Run Sobel X on the diagonal stripes, then Sobel Y. Same image, very different maps. One kernel cannot see everything, which is why layers have dozens of them.
  • Try the noise image with a blur. Then with sharpen. Sharpening noise produces more noise — a filter has no idea what's signal.
  • Draw a thick white line, then a thin one. Compare the Laplacian's response. Kernel size sets the scale of feature a layer can see; a 3×3 kernel is blind to anything wider than three pixels, which is why real networks stack them.
  • Upload a photo of yourself. Sobel X on a face is a genuinely strange and useful thing to look at: it's roughly the first thing every vision model computes.
