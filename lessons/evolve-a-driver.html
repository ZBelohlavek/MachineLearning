Reinforcement learning · a game you can win

Evolve a driver from scratch

No gradients. No backpropagation. No teacher, no labels, no reward function to agonise over. Sixty cars with random brains drive into a wall; the handful that got furthest have their brains copied with small random changes; repeat. Give it a couple of minutes and something is driving the track properly. Then you can race it.

The whole algorithm, in five lines

  1. Make a population of cars, each with a randomly wired brain.
  2. Let them all drive until they crash or run out of time.
  3. Score each one by how far along the track it got.
  4. Keep the best few. Fill the next generation with copies of them, each nudged by a little random noise.
  5. Go back to step 2.

That's neuroevolution, and the striking part is what it never needs. There's no derivative anywhere in it. The network is never told which weight to change or which way to move it. Selection does all of the work, purely by letting bad brains stop reproducing.

What each car can actually perceive

Seven whisker sensors, each reporting how far the road carries on in that direction, plus its own speed. Eight numbers in, two out: how hard to turn and how hard to accelerate. The brain in between is about ninety weights, which is smaller than this paragraph.

That's a brutally limited view of the world. The car can't see the track layout, doesn't know where the finish is, has no memory of the corner it just took, and has never been told what a wall is. Everything it appears to know about racing lives in ninety numbers that were selected rather than designed.

Fitness is reward shaping wearing a different hat

The score here is "distance covered toward the finish, with a bonus for getting there quickly". Every problem from the Rocket League lesson walks straight back in. Score only the finish and nothing ever finishes. Score raw distance travelled and the cars learn to drive in circles forever. Score speed alone and they sprint into the first wall at full throttle. The name changes from reward function to fitness function. The difficulty doesn't.

Evolution or gradient descent?

Evolution (this page)

  • Needs no gradient, so the score can be literally anything, including "did it finish", which isn't differentiable and never will be.
  • Trivially parallel: every car is independent.
  • Barely any maths to get wrong. The algorithm on this page is about forty lines.
  • Scales badly with parameter count. Ninety weights is fine. Ninety million is hopeless.
vs

Policy gradients (Rocket League)

  • Uses every step of every episode instead of just the final score, so it squeezes far more out of the same experience.
  • Follows a derivative, so it scales to networks with billions of weights.
  • Needs a differentiable objective and a lot of careful tuning: clipping, advantages, entropy.
  • Many more ways to fail silently.

Rule of thumb: few parameters and a messy objective favours evolution, many parameters and a dense signal favours gradients. Both are on this site so you can feel the difference rather than read about it. This page learns a driver from one score at the end of each life. The Rocket League agent learns from a number arriving thirty times a second.

Experiments worth running

  • Set mutation to 0.01, then 0.5. Tiny mutations mean the population improves smoothly and then simply stops, unable to reach anything far from where it started. Huge mutations destroy good drivers as fast as they turn up, and the best-car line saws up and down. The useful range is somewhere in between and it's narrower than you'd like.
  • Set survivors to 2%. Only the single best car breeds, so the whole population turns into near-copies of one driver. It converges fast and then can't adapt when you change the track, because the diversity that made progress possible has been bred out.
  • Draw a new corner while it's running. Turn on the brush, add a hairpin, and watch the champions plough straight into it. Their driving was never general skill. It was a habit that fit one specific track.
  • Shrink the population to 10. Each generation runs much faster but samples far less of the space, so progress per generation collapses. Population size is a compute-versus-exploration trade, and it's the same trade as batch size back in the other lessons.
  • Evolve on the Circuit, then switch to the Grand tour without restarting. Watch how much of the skill survives the move. Transfer is a real research problem, and this is the cheapest demonstration of it you'll ever run.
  • Race the champion, and lose. Then watch how it does it: barely steering on the straights, turning hard exactly at the apex, because everything else got selected out generations ago. It isn't driving well. It's driving in the only way that survived.
