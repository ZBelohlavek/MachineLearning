Reinforcement learning · a game you can win

Evolve a driver from scratch

No gradients. No backpropagation. No teacher, no labels and no reward function to tune. Sixty cars with random brains drive into a wall, the handful that got furthest have their brains copied with small random changes, and the whole thing repeats. Within a couple of minutes something is driving the track properly — and then you can race it.

The whole algorithm, in five lines

  1. Make a population of cars, each with a randomly wired brain.
  2. Let them all drive until they crash or run out of time.
  3. Score each one by how far along the track it got.
  4. Keep the best few. Fill the next generation with copies of them, each nudged by a little random noise.
  5. Go back to step 2.

That's neuroevolution, and the striking thing is what it doesn't need. There's no derivative anywhere — the network is never told which weight to change or in which direction. Selection does all the work: bad brains stop reproducing.

What each car can actually perceive

Seven whisker sensors, each reporting how far the road continues in that direction, plus its own speed. Eight numbers in, two out: how hard to turn and how hard to accelerate. The brain between them is about ninety weights — smaller than a paragraph of text.

That is a brutally limited view of the world. The car cannot see the track layout, does not know where the finish is, has no memory of the last corner, and has never been told what a wall is. Everything it appears to know about racing is encoded in ninety numbers that were selected, not designed.

Fitness is reward shaping wearing a different hat

The score here is "distance covered toward the finish, with a bonus for getting there quickly". Every problem from the Rocket League lesson comes straight back: score only the finish and nothing ever finishes; score raw distance travelled and cars learn to drive in circles forever; score speed alone and they sprint into the first wall. The name changes from reward function to fitness function; the difficulty does not.

Evolution or gradient descent?

Evolution (this page)

  • Needs no gradient, so the score can be anything at all — including "did it finish", which is not differentiable.
  • Trivially parallel: every car is independent.
  • Barely any maths to get wrong. This lesson's algorithm is about forty lines.
  • Scales badly with parameter count. Ninety weights, fine. Ninety million, hopeless.
vs

Policy gradients (Rocket League)

  • Uses every step of every episode, not just the final score, so it learns from far less experience.
  • Follows a derivative, so it scales to networks with billions of weights.
  • Needs a differentiable objective and careful tuning — clipping, advantages, entropy.
  • Many more ways to fail silently.

The rule of thumb: few parameters and a messy objective favours evolution; many parameters and a dense signal favours gradients. Both are on this site precisely so you can feel the difference — this page learns a driver from a score at the end of each life, while the Rocket League agent learns from a number arriving thirty times a second.

Experiments worth running

  • Set mutation to 0.01, then 0.5. Tiny mutations mean the population improves smoothly and then stops — it can't reach anything far from where it started. Huge mutations destroy good drivers as fast as they appear, and the best-car line saws up and down. Somewhere in between is where evolution actually works.
  • Set survivors to 2%. Only the single best car breeds, so the entire population becomes near-copies of one driver. It converges fast, and then can't adapt when you change the track — the diversity that made progress possible is gone.
  • Draw a new corner while it's running. Turn on the brush, add a hairpin, and watch the champions crash into the change. Their driving isn't general skill, it's a habit that fit one specific track.
  • Shrink the population to 10. Each generation is much faster but samples far less of the space, so progress per generation collapses. Population size is a compute-versus-exploration trade, and it's the same trade as batch size in the other lessons.
  • Evolve on the Circuit, then switch to the Grand tour without restarting. Watch how much of the skill survives. Transfer is a genuine research problem, and this is the cheapest demonstration of it you'll ever run.
  • Race the champion, and lose. Then look at how it does it: it barely steers on straights and turns hard exactly at the apex, because everything else got selected out. It isn't driving well — it's driving in the only way that survived.
