Learning to be unpredictable

A fighting-game mixup is rock-paper-scissors with consequences: strike, throw or block, and each one loses to something. There is no best move, and an agent that tries to find one gets read and punished. What there is instead is a best mixture — and the algorithm that finds it is the one that beat professional poker players.

Fight it

Pick a move. It picks at the same time, without seeing yours. Strikes beat throws, throws beat blocks, and a block beats a strike at the same height — but a block at the wrong height gets you counter-hit. Play a few dozen rounds and watch the panel on the right work you out.

Why there is no best move

Here is the whole game. Rows are what you do, columns are what it does, and the number is what you gain. It is antisymmetric — your gain is exactly its loss — so there is nothing to negotiate and no outcome that suits you both.

Look for a row that is better than every other row whatever the column. There isn't one, and that is not an accident of these particular numbers: it is what makes the game a game. Any fixed answer you commit to has a reply that beats it.

Regret, and the mixture it implies

After a round you can ask a cheap and very useful question: how much better off would I have been if I had played something else, given what they just did? That difference is regret. Accumulate it for every action across every round, throw away the negatives, and normalise what's left — that is your next mixture.

P(a)  =  regret(a)+  ⁄  Σb regret(b)+

Play the moves you wish you had played, in proportion to how much you wish it. That is the entire algorithm, and it needs no model of the opponent at all.

The part that catches people out

The mixture it plays right now is not the answer, and is often badly exploitable on its own. What converges to the unexploitable strategy is the average of every mixture it has ever played. So the agent samples from its running average rather than from how it currently feels, and the two look quite different early on.

What they converge to

Throws are rare on purpose

The equilibrium throws far less often than it strikes or blocks. That is not a quirk of the solver — it falls out of the payoffs, because a throw loses to both strikes and only beats blocks. Every fighting game player already knows this by feel.

Unexploitable is not the same as winning

The equilibrium mixture cannot be beaten in the long run. It also cannot beat anyone: against a player who only ever blocks high, it breaks exactly even, because it is not trying to punish anything. Being unexploitable means giving nothing away, not taking anything.

The reader is the opposite. It counts what you do and plays the single best answer to it, which is devastating against a habit and worth nothing against a coin flip. And because its answer is a single move rather than a mixture, it is wide open itself — if you notice what it has settled on, you beat it every round until it notices back.

That trade is the whole of competitive game AI. Poker programs choose the safe side and play close to equilibrium, because the exploitable side loses to anyone who adapts back. A human pro chooses the other side, reads the table, and risks being read in turn.

Experiments worth running

  • Pick one move and spam it against the reader. Count how long it takes to start punishing you. It is usually under ten rounds, and the exploitability meter will have seen it coming before the scoreline does.
  • Spam one move against the unexploitable agent instead. You break roughly even forever. It never punishes you, because punishing would mean leaving equilibrium and exposing itself.
  • Try to be random by hand. Watch your own exploitability meter. People are reliably bad at this: we avoid repeats far more than chance does, and that alone is a readable pattern.
  • Beat the reader deliberately. Establish a habit for ten rounds, then switch to the move that beats its answer. This is exactly what a fighting-game player means by conditioning, and it works on the reader precisely because it commits to one reply.
  • Run the self-play chart and watch the orange line. It never settles. The current mixture keeps swinging while the average underneath it converges — which is why the algorithm plays from the average.
