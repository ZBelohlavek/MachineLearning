Reinforcement learning · flagship project

Train a self-play Rocket League bot

Nobody shows this agent a single example of a good shot. It starts out twitching at random in an empty arena, and from one number that ticks up when the ball moves the right way, it has to work out how to drive, how to line up behind the ball, and how to score. You pick the reward. You pick the opponent. Then you watch, frame by frame, and find out what you actually asked for.

Why this can't be taught the normal way

Most machine learning is supervised. You hand the model thousands of examples with the right answer stapled on, and it learns to copy them. That doesn't work here. Nobody can write down the correct steering angle for every possible arrangement of car, ball and opponent, and even if you could, the right move usually only looks right two seconds later when the ball rolls in.

So reinforcement learning throws out the labelled answer and replaces it with something much weaker: a reward. The agent acts, the world moves, a number comes back. Its whole job is figuring out which of the several hundred decisions it just made deserve the credit. Everything below is machinery for doing that without the thing falling apart.

Step 1 · Drive the car yourself

Before you train anything, drive the thing yourself. Arrow keys to steer and accelerate, WASD if you prefer. The important part: you can only turn while you're moving. That's true of real cars and it's also the single detail that makes this problem hard, because standing still is a trap the agent has to learn its way out of.

What the agent actually sees

24 numbers, updated 30× a second

There are no pixels in any of this. The policy never sees the arena above. It sees these 24 numbers and nothing else, thirty times a second. Two decisions in this list are doing most of the work:

  • It's egocentric. "Ball ahead" and "ball left" are measured from the driver's seat, not from the field. A situation that looks the same to the car produces the same numbers no matter where on the pitch it happens, so something learned in one corner shows up in the other three for free.
  • It's side-agnostic. Orange's whole world gets rotated 180° before it reaches the network, so both cars are convinced they're attacking the right-hand goal. That's the trick that lets one network play both sides of a self-play match.
Observation design is where most RL projects are won or lost

Feed the network raw field coordinates instead and it has to learn the same skill four separate times, once per corner. Feed it the right relative quantities and one lesson covers all four. No amount of clever algorithm further down can rescue a badly chosen observation, which is a thing I wish someone had told me earlier.

Step 2 · Design the reward

Here's the part that catches everyone. The agent does not do what you want. It does what you pay it for. Those are the same thing only if you were unusually careful, and you probably weren't.

Scoring is the actual objective, but a goal turns up maybe once in three hundred decisions, and only after a long chain of them went right. You can learn from that alone. It's just extremely slow, and you can watch it be slow later with the "Goals only" preset. So we add shaping terms: small, constant hints pointing roughly the right way. Shaping is how you get an agent off the ground. It's also how you teach it something ridiculous without noticing.

The second run is the same algorithm with a broken reward

Now switch the panel to Side by side. Both of these agents got twelve thousand episodes, the same network, the same physics, the same PPO code. The only thing I changed was the reward: the one on the right was paid generously for touching and approaching the ball, and next to nothing for scoring.

It finishes the run scoring in 0 of 30 empty-net attempts. The well-shaped agent gets 19 of 20. What I didn't expect was how it fails. Measured over 30 episodes it sits 3.4× closer to the ball than the good agent (mean distance 17 units against 58) while touching it 13× less often (0.2 touches per episode against 2.6). It learned to stalk the ball and never make contact, because contact pushes the ball away, and proximity was what I was paying for. Nobody wrote "hover near the ball" anywhere in the reward. It's just the maximum of what I actually specified.

Step 4 · How the learning actually works

The agent is a small network: 24 inputs, two hidden layers of 64 units, 9 outputs. One score per action, from three throttle settings crossed with three steering settings. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities and the agent rolls dice. That randomness isn't sloppiness, it's the only reason it ever finds anything new.

Training uses PPO, which stands for Proximal Policy Optimisation and is the algorithm behind most of the game-playing agents you've read about. Each round does three things:

  1. Play. Run a batch of episodes on the current policy, recording every state, the action taken, and how likely the policy thought that action was at the time.
  2. Judge. A second network, the critic, guesses how much future reward each state was worth. Subtract its guess from what really happened and you get the advantage, which answers "was that better than expected?" rather than "was that good?". Winning from a winning position teaches almost nothing. Scraping a win from a hopeless one teaches a lot.
  3. Improve. Push the network toward the actions that beat expectations, then clip how far it's allowed to move, so one unlucky batch can't fling the policy somewhere it'll never come back from. The clip is the "proximal" part, and it's most of the reason this trains at all.
Everything is running on this page

The arena physics, both networks, backpropagation, Adam, GAE and the PPO update come to about a thousand lines of ordinary JavaScript spread across rocket-env.js, rocket-train.js and nn.js. No server, no pretrained anything. When you press start below, the learning is happening on your machine.

Step 5 · Train it yourself

Now do it for real. Press start and then leave it alone: this is your machine, from random weights, with no help from me. Here's roughly what happens and in what order. The first two phases usually show up inside a minute on a modern laptop. (If you'd rather skip ahead, or you want a decent opponent to play against, "Load trained agent" drops a finished policy straight in.)

  • Twitching (0–30s). Actions are close to uniform noise, the car spins on the spot, and entropy sits near its ceiling of 2.20, which is ln 9: a policy with no opinion whatsoever. Reward hovers around nothing.
  • Ball attraction (30s–2min). Touches per episode lift off the floor and the reward curve turns upward. Approach and touch are the cheapest reward available, so they get collected first. This is shaping doing its job.
  • Direction (2–4min). Touches start becoming shots, and entropy drops under about 1.0 as the policy settles into habits. On one run of this page I got 74% of episodes ending in a goal after three minutes, and it beat the scripted bot 8–6.
  • The curriculum bites. Keep an eye on the difficulty stat. It opens at 30%, with the ball spawning nearby, and only widens once the agent can reliably find it. The moment it hits 100%, touches per episode usually fall for a while. The agent didn't get worse; the exam got harder. A metric dropping because the task changed underneath it is one of the easiest ways to misread a training run, and I've fooled myself with it more than once.
  • Positioning (5min+). The agent starts coming at the ball from the correct side rather than poking it wherever it happens to be pointing. This is the first behaviour that looks deliberate instead of merely enthusiastic.

Self-play has a trap in it

When both cars share a brain, goals scored always roughly equals goals conceded. The agent is playing itself, so the net score tells you nothing no matter how good it gets. Worse, two copies can settle into a stable, mutually awful equilibrium and sit there happily forever. That's why the opponent selector offers a frozen past self (a snapshot from several updates back) and a fixed scripted bot. An opponent that doesn't improve alongside you is the only way to know you're improving.

Experiments worth running

  • Kill the shaping. Load "Goals only" and reset. With reward arriving once every few hundred steps, progress more or less stops. This is the sparse-reward problem, and it's why a surprising amount of RL research is really research about exploration.
  • Turn the curriculum off. Every episode now starts with a full random kickoff instead of a gift-wrapped ball. Time how long the first touches take to appear compared with before.
  • Set the entropy bonus to zero. Sometimes nothing bad happens. Sometimes the policy decides in the first minute that the answer is "always turn left" and never finds out otherwise, because it stopped sampling anything else long enough to check.
  • Push the discount γ down to 0.85. The agent goes short-sighted and takes a touch now over a goal two seconds from now, because two seconds of discounting has made the goal nearly worthless to it.
  • Crank the learning rate to 0.02. Watch the reward curve spike and then come apart. Then go look at the PPO clip slider and work out why it took as long to fall apart as it did.
  • Train against the scripted bot instead of self-play. You'll end up with an agent that handles that one bot nicely and is strangely bad against you. Overfitting isn't only a supervised problem.
