Reinforcement learning · flagship project

Train a self-play Rocket League bot

Nobody will show this agent a single example of a good shot. It starts by twitching at random in an empty arena, and it has to discover — from a number that goes up when the ball moves the right way — how to drive, how to line up, and how to score. You design the reward, you choose the opponent, and you watch it happen frame by frame.

Why this can't be taught the normal way

Most machine learning is supervised: you hand the model thousands of examples with the right answer attached, and it learns to copy them. That approach dies here. Nobody can write down the "correct" steering angle for every possible arrangement of car, ball and opponent — and even if they could, the right move often only reveals itself two seconds later, when the ball rolls into the net.

Reinforcement learning replaces the labelled answer with a much weaker signal: a reward. The agent acts, the world changes, a number comes back. Its job is to work out which of the hundreds of decisions it just made deserve the credit. That's the whole field in one sentence, and everything below is machinery for doing it reliably.

What the agent actually sees

24 numbers, updated 30× a second

There are no pixels here. The policy never sees the picture above — it sees these 24 numbers, and nothing else. Two design decisions in this list do most of the heavy lifting:

  • It's egocentric. "ball ahead" and "ball left" are measured in the car's own frame, not the field's. A situation that looks the same from the driver's seat produces the same numbers wherever on the pitch it happens, so a lesson learned in one corner transfers to the other three.
  • It's side-agnostic. Orange's world is rotated 180° before it hits the network, so both cars believe they are attacking the right-hand goal. That's what lets a single network play both sides of a self-play match.
Observation design is where most RL projects are won or lost

Feed the network raw absolute coordinates and it has to learn the same skill four times over. Feed it the right relative quantities and it can generalise from a fraction of the experience. Nothing in the algorithm below can rescue a bad observation.

Step 2 · Design the reward

Here's the uncomfortable truth about reinforcement learning: the agent does not do what you want. It does what you reward. Those are the same thing only if you were extremely careful.

Scoring is the real objective, but a goal happens maybe once in three hundred decisions, and only after a long chain of them went right. Learning from that signal alone is possible but slow — try the "Goals only" preset later and watch it flounder. So we add shaping terms: small, dense hints that point in roughly the right direction. Shaping is how you get an agent off the ground, and it is also how you accidentally teach it something ridiculous.

Step 4 · How the learning actually works

The agent is a small neural network: 24 inputs, two hidden layers of 64 units, and 9 outputs — one score per action (three throttle settings × three steering settings). Softmax turns those scores into probabilities, and the agent rolls a die. Randomness is not a flaw here; it is the only way it ever discovers anything.

Training uses PPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation), the same family of algorithm behind most of the game-playing agents you've read about. Each round of training does three things:

  1. Play. Run a batch of episodes with the current policy, recording every state, the action taken, and the probability the policy assigned to it at the time.
  2. Judge. A second network — the critic — predicts how much total future reward each state was worth. Subtracting its prediction from what actually happened gives the advantage: not "was this good?" but "was this better than expected?" That distinction is what makes the signal usable. Winning from a great position teaches nothing; winning from a hopeless one teaches a lot.
  3. Improve. Nudge the network to make positive-advantage actions more likely — but clip the change, so a single noisy batch can never yank the policy somewhere it can't recover from. That clip is the "proximal" in PPO, and it's the difference between training that converges and training that explodes.

Step 5 · Train it yourself

Now do it for real. Press start and leave it running: this is your machine, from random weights, with no help. Below is what to look for, roughly in the order it happens — expect the first two phases within a minute or so on a modern laptop. (In a hurry, or want a strong opponent to play against? "Load trained agent" drops the finished policy straight in.)

  • Twitching (0–30s). Actions are near-uniform noise, the car spins on the spot, and entropy sits near its maximum of 2.20 (that's ln 9, a policy with no opinion at all). Reward hovers around zero.
  • Ball attraction (30s–2min). "Touches per episode" lifts off the floor and the reward curve turns upward. The approach and touch terms are the cheapest reward on offer, so they get collected first — this is reward shaping doing its job.
  • Direction (2–4min). Touches start turning into shots and entropy falls below about 1.0 as the policy commits to habits. On a recent run this page reached 74% of episodes ending in a goal after three minutes, and beat the scripted bot 8–6.
  • The curriculum bites. Watch the "difficulty" stat. It starts at 30% — the ball spawns close by — and only widens once the agent reliably finds it. When it reaches 100%, touches per episode usually drop for a while: the problem just got harder, not the agent worse. A metric falling because the task changed underneath it is one of the most common ways to misread a training run.
  • Positioning (5min+). The agent starts approaching from the correct side instead of nudging the ball wherever it happens to be facing — the first behaviour that looks genuinely deliberate rather than merely eager.

Self-play has a trap in it

When both cars share one brain, "goals scored" is always about equal to "goals conceded" — the agent is playing itself, so the net score is meaningless no matter how good it gets. Worse, two agents can drift into a stable, mutually terrible equilibrium and never leave. That's why the opponent selector offers a frozen past self (a snapshot from several updates ago) and a fixed scripted bot: an opponent that doesn't move with you is the only way to know you're improving.

Experiments worth running

  • Kill the shaping. Load the "Goals only" preset and reset. With a reward that arrives once every few hundred steps, progress nearly stops. This is the sparse-reward problem, and it's why so much RL research is really about exploration.
  • Turn the curriculum off. The agent then starts every episode with a full random kickoff instead of near the ball. Compare how long the first touches take to appear.
  • Set the entropy bonus to zero. Sometimes it's fine. Sometimes the policy locks onto "always turn left" in the first minute and never escapes, because it stopped sampling anything else long enough to find out there was better.
  • Push the discount γ down to 0.85. The agent becomes short-sighted: it will take a touch now over a goal in two seconds, because two seconds of discounting has made the goal nearly worthless.
  • Crank the learning rate to 0.02. Watch the reward curve spike and then fall apart. Then look at the PPO clip slider and try to work out why it doesn't fall apart immediately.
  • Train against the scripted bot instead of self-play. You'll get an agent that beats that specific bot and is oddly bad against you. Overfitting isn't only a supervised problem.
