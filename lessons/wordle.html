Guessing with information

Every other lesson here trains something. This one does not, because the best known way to play Wordle is not learned from data at all — it is calculated. Before you type a word you can work out exactly how much it will tell you, in bits, and then say the word that tells you the most. It is worth seeing an idea this good that has no network in it anywhere.

Race the solver

You and the solver get the same hidden word. Type a guess and it plays its own turn immediately, out of the same list, with no knowledge of the answer. Fewer guesses wins.

After each of its guesses the solver lists how many words are still possible. Watch that number: a good guess does not just eliminate a few words, it cuts the list by something like a factor of sixty.

What a guess is actually worth

Type any word into the box below. The chart shows every colouring that word could come back as, and how many of the remaining answers would produce each one. Those bars are the whole idea: each bar is a group you would still have to tell apart afterwards.

A guess that produces one enormous bar has told you almost nothing — whatever happens, you are left with nearly the same list. A guess that shatters the list into many small bars has done a lot of work, because whichever bar you land in, there is not much left in it.

Best guesses right now

by expected information

Recomputed from whatever is still possible, so this list changes as the game does. A word marked "can't win" is one the colours have already ruled out — worth saying anyway when it splits the survivors better than any word that could win.

bits(guess)  =  Σcolourings   p · log2( 1 / p )

Read it right to left. If a colouring holds a fraction p of the candidates, then seeing it shrinks your list by a factor of 1/p, and log₂ of that factor is the number of times you have halved the problem — its bits. Multiply by how likely that colouring is, add up over all of them, and you have what the guess is worth on average before you have said it.

With 694 words in play you start with 9.4 bits of uncertainty, because that is how many times you would have to halve the list to get to one word. Every guess is an attempt to buy as many of those bits as possible.

So what should you open with?

Nothing is known at the start, so the best opening word is a fixed property of the word list — the same every game. The table below is computed here, in your browser, by scoring every word against every word: about half a million comparisons, which takes roughly a tenth of a second.

Greedy is very good and not quite right

The solver above is greedy: at every turn it takes whatever buys the most bits right now, without checking where that leaves it two turns later. I ran it against all 694 possible answers, which is the only honest way to talk about how well it plays.

Solving every word in the list, a few at a time, so the page stays usable while it runs…

The interesting row is the second one. TRACE buys the most information of any opening word and gives the best average. CRANE buys slightly less and gives a slightly worse average — but it never needs more than four guesses, while TRACE occasionally needs five.

That is not a rounding error, it is the difference between two things you might want. Maximising expected information optimises the mean. If what you actually care about is never having a bad day, you want the worst case, and the word that wins on one does not have to win on the other. Picking the metric before you optimise it is most of the job, and this is the cheapest demonstration of it on the site.

Experiments worth running

  • Open with a word full of repeats. Try VIVID or FUNNY. Watch the bar chart collapse into a few tall columns: a repeated letter spends one of your five questions asking something you have already asked.
  • Play a word that cannot possibly be the answer. Partway through a game, look down the ranked list for a row marked "can't win" that still scores well. Saying it throws away a chance to finish this turn and often saves a turn later.
  • Find a guess that scores zero bits. Once only one candidate remains, every guess is worth nothing, because the answer is already determined. Information is about what you do not yet know, not about how clever the word is.
  • Watch the "still possible" count after the solver's first guess. It goes from several hundred to single figures surprisingly often. Six bits is a factor of sixty-four.
  • Beat the solver. You can, and not only by luck: it never considers that the answer is more likely to be a common word, because this list does not carry that information. A person's sense of which words are plausible is real information the solver does not have.
