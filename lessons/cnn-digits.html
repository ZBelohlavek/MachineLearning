Build and train a digit CNN

Last lesson you picked the kernel numbers yourself. Here the network picks them, by being wrong ten thousand times and adjusting a little after each one. Train it in your browser, then draw a digit with your mouse and watch it move through every layer.

The data, made from nothing

There's no dataset to download. Every training image below is drawn by your browser on the spot: a random digit in a random font, random size, rotated a bit, shifted a bit, with a dusting of noise on top. Press "New data" for a fresh batch.

Training images · 20×20 pixels

1,000 for training, 300 held out for testing
Why bother randomising the fonts?

Because a model only ever learns the variation it's shown. Train on one font and you get something that recognises that font. It will fail on your handwriting and it won't warn you, because your test set has precisely the same blind spot as your training set. Turn the augmentation slider to zero, train to a beautiful number, then try drawing a digit. It's the fastest way to feel that failure rather than nod along to it.

The architecture

Two convolutional layers, each followed by ReLU and 2×2 max pooling, then two fully connected layers ending in ten scores, one per digit. This is the standard shape of a small image classifier and has been since the 1990s.

Watch the trade it makes going left to right: the image shrinks (20×20 → 10×10 → 5×5) while the stack of channels gets deeper. It's swapping where something is for what it is. By the last pooling layer, position is almost gone and identity is almost all that's left.

Train it

Press train. Test accuracy usually clears 90% within a few seconds and keeps going. The whole model is a few hundred thousand multiply-adds per image, which your laptop finds trivial.

Confidence

These ten numbers are a softmax over the final layer's scores, so they're forced to add up to 100%. Keep that in mind, because it means the model has no way to tell you "that isn't a digit". Draw a spiral and it will still confidently name one. Calibrated uncertainty is a hard and generally unsolved problem, and this is the cheapest demonstration of why.

Inside the layers

These update live as you draw. The purple tiles are the actual 3×3 kernels the first layer learned. Put them next to the hand-written Sobel kernels from the previous lesson. Nobody told this network to build edge detectors; gradient descent found them because they're the most useful thing a 3×3 window can compute.

Layer 1 responds to strokes: an edge here, a corner there. Layer 2 is much harder to read by eye, and that's the honest situation rather than a failure of this page. Its units respond to combinations of layer 1's outputs, and in bigger networks the deeper layers get steadily less interpretable. A great deal of research exists purely to work out what these pictures mean.

Experiments worth running

  • Drop layer 1 to two filters. Two 3×3 patterns isn't enough vocabulary to describe ten digits, and the accuracy ceiling falls hard. Capacity has a floor as well as a risk.
  • Set augmentation to 0, train to 99%, then draw a digit yourself. The test accuracy stays gorgeous while the predictions on your handwriting fall apart. Your test set came from the same narrow distribution as your training set, so it certified nothing at all.
  • Set augmentation to 2 and train. Accuracy drops and gets much more honest, and drawn digits work noticeably better. Harder training data, better model.
  • Set batch size to 1. Every single image yanks the weights around on its own. The accuracy curve gets visibly noisier, and that noise is the variance a batch was quietly averaging away.
  • Set the learning rate to 0.02. Watch the loss refuse to settle and the learned kernels degenerate into static.
  • Draw a digit in a corner instead of the middle. Convolution is translation equivariant, so the features shift along with the digit, but those two dense layers at the end are not. This is exactly why augmentation with random shifts exists.
