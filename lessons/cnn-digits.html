Build and train a digit CNN

In the last lesson you chose the kernel numbers yourself. Here the network chooses them — by being wrong ten thousand times and adjusting. Train it in your browser, then draw a digit with your mouse and watch it flow through every layer.

The data, made from nothing

There's no dataset to download. Every training image below is drawn by your browser: a random digit in a random font, at a random size, rotated a little, shifted a little, with a dusting of noise. Press "New data" to make a fresh batch.

Training images · 20×20 pixels

1,000 for training, 300 held out for testing
Why bother randomising the fonts?

Because a model only ever learns the variation it sees. Train on one font and you get something that recognises that font — it will fail on your handwriting and you'll have no warning, because your test set has the same blind spot as your training set. Turning the augmentation slider down to zero and then trying to draw a digit is the fastest way to feel what that failure is like.

The architecture

Two convolutional layers, each followed by ReLU and 2×2 max pooling, then two fully connected layers ending in ten scores — one per digit. This is the standard shape of a small image classifier, and it has been the standard shape since the 1990s.

Notice the trade the network makes as you go right: the image gets smaller (20×20 → 10×10 → 5×5) while the stack of channels gets deeper. It is trading where something is for what it is. By the final pooling layer, position is nearly gone and identity is nearly all that's left.

Train it

Press train. Test accuracy usually passes 90% within a few seconds and keeps climbing — the whole thing is a few hundred thousand multiply-adds per image, which your laptop finds easy.

Confidence

These ten numbers are a softmax over the final layer's scores, so they're forced to add up to 100%. That is worth remembering: the model cannot tell you "this is not a digit". Draw a spiral and it will still confidently pick one. Calibrated uncertainty is a hard, unsolved-in-general problem, and this is the demonstration of why.

Inside the layers

These update live as you draw. The purple tiles are the actual 3×3 kernels the first layer learned — compare them with the hand-written Sobel kernels from the previous lesson. Nobody told the network to build edge detectors; gradient descent found them because they are the most useful thing a 3×3 window can compute.

Layer 1 responds to strokes: an edge here, a corner there. Layer 2 is harder to read by eye, and that's the honest situation — its units respond to combinations of layer 1's outputs, and in bigger networks the deeper layers get less and less interpretable. A great deal of research exists purely to explain what these pictures mean.

Experiments worth running

  • Drop layer 1 to two filters. Two 3×3 patterns is not enough vocabulary to describe ten digits, and the accuracy ceiling drops hard. Capacity has a floor as well as a risk.
  • Set augmentation to 0, train to 99%, then draw a digit yourself. The test accuracy stays beautiful and the predictions on your handwriting fall apart. Your test set was drawn from the same narrow distribution as your training set, so it certified nothing.
  • Set augmentation to 2 and train. Accuracy is lower and much more honest, and drawn digits work better. Harder training data, better model.
  • Set batch size to 1. Every image yanks the weights around individually. The accuracy curve gets visibly noisier — that's the variance a batch is averaging away.
  • Set the learning rate to 0.02. Watch the loss refuse to settle, and the learned kernels turn into noise.
  • Draw a digit in a corner instead of the middle. Convolution is translation equivariant — the features shift with the digit — but those two dense layers at the end are not. This is exactly why data augmentation with random shifts exists.
