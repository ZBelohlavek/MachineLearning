Drafting from data

Twenty-four champions, each with a strength, a handful of team-mates they work well with, and a handful of opponents they beat. None of that is written down anywhere the model can see. It gets ten picks and a win or a loss, twenty thousand times, and has to work the rest out — and because I wrote the hidden numbers myself, this is the one lesson here where you can mark its homework.

Draft against it

You pick, it picks, five each. It chooses whatever its model says raises its win probability most — and its model is only what it learned from match results. When both teams are full, the real simulator decides, using the hidden numbers neither of you was shown.

      Model

      One weight per champion is not enough

      The obvious model gives every champion a number and adds them up: positive for your team, negative for theirs. It is fast, it is interpretable, and it cannot express the one thing this data is actually made of. "These two are strong together" has nowhere to live when each champion owns exactly one weight, independent of who else is picked.

      A hidden layer can represent that, because a hidden unit can respond to combinations. So the comparison below is not really about model size. It is about whether the shape of the model can hold the shape of the truth.

      Accuracy is the least interesting column. Both models get a good share of winners right, because plenty of matches are decided by one team simply having stronger champions. The column that matters is the last one: how closely the predicted probability tracks the real one. That is only measurable because the real one exists, and on almost every real problem it does not.

      Did it actually find the synergies?

      Marking the homework. Asking "does it rate the good pairs highly" turns out not to settle anything: both models do, because a champion who appears in a lot of synergy pairs simply wins more often, and a single weight is perfectly capable of absorbing that. The question has to be sharper.

      So measure the interaction directly, the way you would in an experiment. Hold the rest of both teams fixed and take a difference of differences: the prediction with both champions, minus the prediction with only the first, minus the prediction with only the second, plus the prediction with neither. Everything that depends on one champion alone cancels. What is left is the part of the prediction that needs both at once.

      Measured interaction

      pairs it was never told about

      For the linear model this is not small, it is zero — the terms cancel algebraically, no matter what it learned. The network reports a clear positive interaction for the real pairs and a negative one for the rest, from nothing but which teams happened to win.

      Predicted against actual

      Every held-out match: the model's guess across, the truth up. A perfect model puts everything on the diagonal. The linear model makes a fat cloud; the network pulls it towards the line without ever reaching it, because some of the variation is luck that nothing could predict.

      The last number is the one I would show a sceptic. Across every pair, the size of the interaction the network reports tracks the size of the real one — not perfectly, but far too well for chance. It rebuilt a piece of the rulebook from results alone.

      Two ways to stop it memorising

      Capacity is not free. It is a bet that you have enough data to pay for it, and on six thousand matches the bet fails outright. Here is the same network on six thousand, with only the regularisation changed:

      Forty thousand steps each, same seed, same six thousand matches, scored on the same held-out set. The linear model on this data lands at 0.618.

      Read the top row carefully. The training loss is 0.101 and the held-out loss is 2.443 — it has not failed to learn, it has learned the wrong thing extremely well. It knows which exact compositions won and has nothing to say about any other. The best held-out score in that run happened at step 2,000 and every step after it made things worse.

      The two fixes are different in kind. Weight decay pushes back on the model. Training on unfinished drafts instead adds data — the same matches, seen at every stage of the draft — and it was the bigger of the two, which I did not expect, since I added it to fix something else entirely. That is the ordinary shape of this work: the thing you change for one reason turns out to matter for another, and you only find out because you measured.

      Either way, six thousand matches leaves the network barely ahead of the model with one weight per champion. Twenty thousand is where the gap opens. Turn the settings down below and watch the held-out curve peel away from the training curve.

      Experiments worth running

      • Draft with the linear model as your opponent. It picks strong champions and ignores who they are playing next to, which is beatable once you notice.
      • Pick a known counter deliberately. Look at what the model expects, then pick against its last champion. Watch the real odds bar move differently from its prediction.
      • Drop to six thousand matches, turn off unfinished drafts, and set weight decay to 1e-4. That is the top row of the table, and you can watch the orange curve turn.
      • Then put weight decay back to 1e-3 without touching anything else. Same data, same architecture, and the curve stops turning.
      • Compare accuracy with correlation. Accuracy barely separates the two models; correlation with the true probability separates them clearly. Pick your metric carefully.
